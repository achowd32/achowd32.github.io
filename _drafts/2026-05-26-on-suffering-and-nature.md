Every reasonable person I've encountered is of the mind that suffering is bad; crucially, most people extend this to non-human suffering as well. Even the most fervent steak-enthusiast will tell you that she would rather an individual cow live a happy life rather than die miserably, all else held equal (i.e. she won't get to eat the cow). We see this propensity to care about animal suffering everywhere in society — people are quick to hurl vile insults at the perpatrator in the latest animal abuse case, and routinely avert their gaze from displays of horrific violence even in the natural world. Perhaps the existence of online spaces like <a href="https://www.reddit.com/r/natureismetal/">r/natureismetal</a> casts doubt on the veracity of my claim, but I maintain that a majority of people are horrified and saddened upon seeing a deer gruesomely mauled alive by a lion, even if a few of them watch on with (what I assume to be) morbid curiosity.

In parallel, I want to illuminate another worldview that lurks in the human spirit: the propensity to romanticize nature. Who among us hasn't been moved by the sheer beauty we see when we journey into the mountains, the woods, or the beaches? Many of my favorite artworks are landscape paintings, and indeed the fame of landscape painters and movements like the Hudson River School are further evidence of this kind of romanticization of nature. And it makes complete sense that we should find such things beautiful — we evolved out of these very environments and lived among them for millennia. There even seems to be <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8125471/#sec3-ijerph-18-04790">empirical evidence</a> that being in nature is good for you, if any of my fellow Computer Science/Engineering majors had any doubt.

What I find particularly fascinating about these two tendencies — aversion to suffering and attraction to nature — is that they seem to be deeply embedded in most people, and yet they exist in <i>complete conflict with one another</i>! If you've caught on to the obvious point that suffering is everywhere in nature, you might be jumping to point out that one can appreciate nature as divorced from suffering; that is to say, one may appreciate those parts of nature that are devoid of suffering. But this point, while not entirely wrong, holds much less weight when the proper relationship between nature and suffering is brought to light.

The key point is not merely that suffering abounds in nature, but rather that suffering is <i>embedded in nature at its very core</i>. <a href="https://www.youtube.com/@CosmicSkeptic">Alex O'Connor (of CosmicSkeptic fame)</a> has a line he often delivers as part of his "Argument from Animal Suffering" which I've taken a liking to: "suffering is the very engine of evolution". His point being, of course, that the very mechanism by which living beings propagate is driven by suffering. The modern gazelle is only able to run so fast because millions of its slower predecessors were violently killed by cheetahs.

But if this relationship of suffering to evolution feels unconcerning, likely due to the abstract character of "evolution" as a process or scientific principle, fret not: I think Alex's principle can be extended from evolution to the natural world more concretely. The steady state of so many natural systems can only be maintained through a myriad of suffering-intense processes. To illustrate the point, it is worth considering a few examples:

1. In any stable food chain, we expect predators to hunt and kill their prey, generation after generation. If the prey species is not condemned to a life of fear and the possibility of death at the hands of a predator, their population will balloon out of control, and wreak ecological havoc.
2. Forest fires are natural processes that are <a href="https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/ecological-benefits-fire/">healthy</a>, in the long term, for the forest. To maintain long term ecological balance, fires must periodically devastate parts of a forest and upend the lives of animals who had settled there.
3. If one is to believe <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R/K_selection_theory">r/K selection theory</a>, one should also take into account the misery abundant in the life of an r-selector. The fruit fly that lays 500 eggs should expect most of its offspring to die within days of being born, whether from predation, starvation, or other environmental conditions.

These are not instances of suffering that happen, incidentally, to be a part of nature. They are <i>prerequisites for the existence of nature as we know it</i>. Without the routine occurrence of the above phenomena, "nature" would be an altogether different thing.

That they can simultaneously exist in so many people is a testament to just how subtly insidious our nature truly is.
