Nostalgia and heartbreak are two of my favorite emotions. This is true rationally (as in I enjoy thinking about these emotions in a more intellectual sense) and even experentially (as in I find considerable satisfaction in experiencing these otherwise quite painful feelings). <elaborate>

As you might expect, it's quite difficult to explain exactly why. Neither of these seem closely related to any other sensation we might typically regard as "positive", such as happiness or love. Despite these difficulties, I suspect many of you won't be at all shocked by my love for nostalgia or heartbreak; I even suspect many of you will agree with me quite strongly. After all, countless great works of art explore the incessant, haunting nature of nostalgia for young adults; many more tales of love and loss have been created in the wake of the artist's own heartbreak. With my primary mode of artistic consumption being film, it is quite telling to me that the list of <a href="https://letterboxd.com/official/list/top-250-films-with-the-most-fans/">top 250 films with the most fans on Letterboxd</a> has its third and fourth spots claimed by two films which grapple extensively with both nostalgia and heartbreak: <em>La La Land</em> and <em>Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind</em>. There is quite clearly something about these feelings that draws people, that makes people want to spend hours of their time watching and rewatching media that awakens and feeds these feelings.

I'm no different: my own <a href="https://letterboxd.com/thedonuthole/">Letterboxd top four</a> (as it currently stands) features both <em>Chungking Express</em> and <em>Only Yesterday</em>. The former takes a powerfully bittersweet look at loneliness, love, and heartbreak, while the latter is a slow, reflective movie whose protagonist spends most of her time pontificating on her nostalgic memories of childhood. I must confess that although I will spend a considerable chunk of this post analyzing these films, they are fundamentally explanatory weapons in the way my words likely won’t ever be. If I ever want to communicate to someone the melancholy bliss inherent in nostalgia, simply showing them <em>Only Yesterday</em> would likely trump any understanding I could inculcate with words alone.

Perhaps the most successful attempt at verbally capturing the mood and themes of Chungking Express that I've seen is featured in <a href="https://letterboxd.com/dirkh/film/chungking-express/">this Letterboxd review</a>: "I love the smallness of the biggest emotion we know." The film instills the most mundane moments in the lives of the most ordinary people with incredible emotional richness, helped along by the tender score and the visual poetry of every shot. Characters in <em>Chungking Express</em> don't shy away from being dramatic, or even overdramatic, but choose to revel in it.

There is a reason I opened this post with a quote from Cop 223 choosing to be a heartbroken idiot, buying nearly expired cans of pineapple for no good reason other than adding an emotional heaviness to his life as he mourns his breakup and the loss of his love. Infinite moments like these are scattered throughout the film: Cop 663 talking to the bar of soap in his apartment, Cop 223 being wished happy birthday by a stranger whose name he doesn't know, Faye cleaning her love's apartment because she isn't able to confess her feelings directly.

The characters and the film itself aren't afraid to take moments in their life that are foolish or pathetic or even trivial, and bathe in their richness and their glory. The stark contrast between the emotional power of these moments as presented by the film and the absurd frivolity of these moments when thought of as lived experiences is what makes <em>Chungking Express</em> so great. <em>This</em> is the "smallness of the biggest emotion": the often confusing disparity between emotions as felt and emotions as rationalised is beautified in the film. While certain small experiences feel like they should inspire a thoroughly dispassionate response, we often can't help but feel these big, powerful, grand emotions because of them.
